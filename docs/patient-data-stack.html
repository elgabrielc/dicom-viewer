ii. Privacy Rule

HIPAA — the right to access begins here.

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 is the substrate of US health data law. Two main rules implement it: the Privacy Rule (Subparts A and E of 45 CFR Part 164) and the Security Rule (Subpart C).

Designated record set means medical records, billing records, and any records used to make decisions about you. The category is broad on purpose — imaging is included.

Boundaries of the designated record set

What's in: medical records, billing records, claims, enrollment data, and any records used to make decisions about the individual.

What's not: information compiled in anticipation of litigation; QA/peer review/credentialing; research records during research; raw device data not in the record; psychotherapy notes (separate carve-out at 164.524(a)(1)(i)).

Personal representatives — 164.502(g)

Same access rights as the patient: HCP holders, court-appointed guardians, parents of unemancipated minors (with state-law variations for sensitive services), and executors of estates.

Business Associate Agreements

Required between covered entities and business associates. Must include permitted uses, safeguards, subcontractor flow-down, breach notification, and termination. For health tech: if you handle PHI on behalf of a covered entity, you're a BA.

iii. Security Rule

The HIPAA Security Rule — safeguarding ePHI.

Codified at 45 CFR Part 164 Subpart C. Separate from the Privacy Rule but equally important. Most OCR enforcement actions cite Security Rule violations.

A.

Administrative

§ 164.308

Security management, workforce training, contingency plans, BAA contracts, access management.

P.

Physical

§ 164.310

Facility access controls, workstation security, device and media controls.

T.

Technical

§ 164.312

Access controls, audit logs, integrity controls, transmission security (encryption).

The Security Risk Analysis (164.308(a)(1)) is the foundational compliance step — an ongoing assessment of where ePHI lives, what threats exist, and how risks are mitigated. Failure to conduct or document one is the most commonly cited Security Rule violation in OCR enforcement actions.

Required vs Addressable: Required must be implemented. Addressable requires the entity to assess and document why not, if skipping. "Addressable" does not mean optional.

iv. The full toolkit

Most people only know about access. HIPAA actually grants six distinct patient rights under the Privacy Rule.

v.

§ 164.522(b)

vi.

NPP

§ 164.520

Receive Notice of Privacy Practices at first encounter.

v. Layered on top

HITECH — privacy meets the digital era.

The Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act of 2009, part of ARRA, modernized HIPAA for electronic records:

  • Explicit right to receive electronic copies when records are kept electronically
  • Stiffened penalties — up to $50,000 per violation, $1.5M annual cap per identical violation
  • Business Associates directly liable under HIPAA
  • Breach Notification Rule — 60-day window; OCR breach portal for incidents affecting 500+ individuals
  • Meaningful Use incentives drove EHR adoption (later renamed Promoting Interoperability under MIPS)
vi. Modern infrastructure

The 21st Century Cures Act — portability as policy.

Passed in 2016 with bipartisan support. Implementation through the ASTP/ONC Cures Act Final Rule (May 2020), amended by HTI-1 (January 2024) and HTI-2 (December 2024).

A note on naming. ONC was elevated and renamed in mid-2024 to ASTP/ONC (Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy / Office of the National Coordinator). The acronym ONC remains in common use; both refer to the same body.

viii. Population API

Bulk FHIR — the population sibling.

Also called "Flat FHIR." Part of the same certification criterion as the Patient Access API but designed for population-level queries.

  • Asynchronous bulk export
  • Output: line-delimited JSON (NDJSON)
  • Used for HEDIS quality reporting, ACO analytics, public health, research
  • Authorization: SMART Backend Services (asymmetric key, no interactive user)
  • Specification: hl7.org/fhir/uv/bulkdata
ix. Cultural shift

The OpenNotes mandate.

The information blocking rule and Cures Act together effectively require providers to share clinical notes with patients without delay. Since April 5, 2021, patients have immediate portal access to:

  • Clinical notes (the eight USCDI note types and more)
  • Test results (including pathology and imaging reports)
  • Discharge summaries
  • Consultation notes
Clinicians now write knowing patients will read. Documentation style has shifted. Withholding sensitive results pending physician conversation is generally non-compliant.
x. AI regulation

Predictive DSI — AI transparency in certified EHRs.

New in HTI-1 (2024), codified at 45 CFR 170.315(b)(11). Certified EHRs that incorporate AI/algorithmic decision support must provide source attribute information about those models:

  • Training data characteristics
  • Intended use
  • Fairness considerations
  • Validation methods
  • Risk management practices

The first federal certification requirement specifically targeting AI in healthcare data systems. Significant for the rapidly expanding ambient scribe market (Abridge, Nuance DAX Copilot, Suki, Augmedix, DeepScribe) and AI-drafted clinical content. Implementation is just beginning.

xi. Backstop

Information Blocking — the anti-stonewall rule.

Codified at 45 CFR Part 171. Prohibits practices interfering with EHI access, exchange, or use.

The eight defined exceptions

  • Preventing Harm — practice reasonably necessary to prevent harm
  • Privacy — to protect a person's privacy
  • Security — to protect information security
  • Infeasibility — when fulfilling is genuinely infeasible
  • Health IT Performance — temporary downtime for maintenance
  • Content and Manner — alternative form/format when initial unable
  • Fees — fees that comply with conditions in the rule
  • Licensing — licensing of interoperability elements with conditions

Enforcement

  • Developers and HIEs/HINs: HHS OIG — CMPs up to $1,000,000 per violation
  • Providers: CMS through Medicare program disincentives (finalized 2024)
  • Complaints: healthit.gov/topic/information-blocking
xii. Payer-side

The CMS Patient Access Rule.

Parallel rule for payers. CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule (2020) + Prior Authorization Final Rule (2024). Applies to Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, CHIP, federal Marketplace plans.

  • Patient Access API — claims + encounters + clinical data the plan holds
  • Provider Directory API
  • Payer-to-Payer API
  • Prior Authorization API — added by 2024 rule
xiii. Financial data

Price Transparency & No Surprises Act.

Separate from clinical data flows but parallel patient-facing regimes around cost:

  • Hospital Price Transparency Rule (2021) — hospitals must publish machine-readable files of standard charges plus consumer-friendly shoppable services display
  • No Surprises Act (2022) — surprise billing protections; good-faith estimates required for self-pay; advanced EOB requirement (delayed)
  • Transparency in Coverage Rule — payers publish machine-readable files of in-network rates and out-of-network allowed amounts
xiv. Additional layer

42 CFR Part 2 — stricter rules for SUD records.

For substance use disorder treatment records from Part 2-covered programs:

  • Higher consent standard (historically per-disclosure)
  • Re-disclosure prohibition
  • February 2024 amendments aligned with HIPAA — single consent for TPO permitted; fully effective February 2026
xv. Additional layer

GINA — genetic information protections.

  • Title I: Health insurers cannot use genetic information for coverage decisions
  • Title II: Employers (15+ employees) cannot use genetic information in employment decisions
  • Section 1557 of the ACA also prohibits discrimination in federally-funded health programs

xviii. Authorization flow

What happens when a patient "connects an app".

The OAuth 2.0 + PKCE flow for patient apps. Eight discrete steps, executing in seconds.

1
App initiates authorization request
App opens browser to EHR's authorize endpoint with client_id, redirect_uri, scope, state, code_challenge.
app → ehr
2
User authenticates with EHR
Patient logs in with portal credentials. App never sees the password.
user ↔ ehr
3
EHR shows consent screen
"App X is requesting access to your medications, conditions, lab results. Allow?"
user ↔ ehr
4
EHR redirects back with authorization code
Short-lived code sent via redirect_uri. Tied to the code_challenge.
ehr → app
5
App exchanges code for tokens
POST to token endpoint with code + code_verifier. Returns access_token + refresh_token.
app → ehr
6
App calls FHIR resources with access token
GET /Patient/{id}, GET /Observation?patient={id}, etc.
app → ehr
7
EHR returns FHIR JSON
FHIR R4 resources per US Core profiles.
ehr → app
8
Refresh as needed
Access token expires; app uses refresh_token. Continues until patient revokes.
app → ehr

xx. Coded values

Terminologies — what makes data actually interoperable.

FHIR is the envelope; terminologies are what make the data computable across systems. Without them, structured fields are not actually exchangeable.

A hemoglobin result is LOINC 718-7, not just the text "hemoglobin." Real interoperability happens at the coded-value level.
LOINC
Lab tests & observations

Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes. Steward: Regenstrief Institute. Free.

SNOMED CT
Clinical concepts

Diagnoses, procedures, body sites, findings. Steward: SNOMED International (paid US license via NLM).

RxNorm
Medications

Standard naming for clinical drugs. Steward: NLM. Free.

Diagnoses, procedures

Clinical Modification for diagnoses; Procedure Coding System for inpatient procedures. Steward: CDC/CMS.

CPT
Procedures (billing)

Current Procedural Terminology. Outpatient procedures. Steward: AMA (paid license).

Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System. Steward: CMS.

NDC
Drug products

National Drug Code. Specific drug products at package level. Steward: FDA.

Unified Code for Units of Measure. The "g/dL" in lab values. Steward: Regenstrief.

xxi. Still dominant

HL7 v2, CCDA, DIRECT — the standards FHIR hasn't replaced.

FHIR is the future direction; these are still the present reality for most data movement in healthcare.

HL7 v2
Health Level Seven v2
The workhorse since the 1990s. Pipe-delimited messages over TCP/IP. Still dominant for ADT, lab interfaces, radiology orders, billing feeds.
CCDA
Consolidated Clinical Document Architecture
XML-based clinical documents. Drives provider-to-provider exchange — Care Everywhere is largely CCDA under the hood. The "PDF of structured healthcare."
DIRECT
Direct Secure Messaging
Healthcare-specific encrypted email. Provider-to-provider referrals; discharge information. Required for ONC certification. Administered by DirectTrust.

xxiii. The imaging gap

DICOM — the parallel universe next to the EHR.

Medical imaging is governed by an entirely different standard, lives in entirely different infrastructure, and is mandated by entirely different (or absent) federal rules.

The single biggest gap in the current patient data framework. The technical standard exists. The regulatory mandate doesn't.

The PACS / EHR divide

  • EHR stores the clinical record — orders, reports, notes
  • PACS stores the actual pixel data — CT slices, MRI volumes
  • The EHR holds a pointer/accession number; pixel data lives in PACS
  • Common PACS vendors: Sectra, Visage, GE, Philips, Fujifilm Synapse

Why no certified API for DICOM

  • File sizes: 0.5–3 GB per study; lifetime archives 50–200 GB
  • System separation from EHR certification
  • No regulatory mandate in HTI-1/HTI-2
  • Existing alternatives (CDs, portals) considered sufficient

Privacy gap

HIPAA stops applying

Once data is in a patient-authorized app, you're generally not a covered entity or BA. Governed instead by:

  • FTC Act (Section 5) — unfair/deceptive practices
  • FTC Health Breach Notification Rule
  • State health-data laws (WA, CA, NV, CT, OR)
  • General privacy laws (CCPA/CPRA, CDPA, CPA)

xxxiv. Audience: Small practices

For small private practice providers.

Compliance

Under HIPAA

  • Privacy Officer + Security Officer designated
  • Security Risk Analysis (164.308(a)(1))
  • Administrative, physical, technical safeguards
  • 30-day access response (or shorter state)
  • BAAs with business associates
  • Workforce training
Pitfalls

What to avoid

  • Excessive fees — $6.50 electronic safe harbor is safest
  • Requiring in-person pickup when electronic is feasible
  • Overly broad authorizations for self-direct access
  • Delays past 30 days without written extension
  • Withholding clinical notes — OpenNotes mandate generally prohibits
Mitigations

Practical resources

Choose an EHR that handles certification machinery. Engage compliance consultant. Cyber insurance with healthcare-specific coverage. MGMA resources. Outsource records request handling (ChartRequest, MRO) if volume is high.

xxxvi. Clinical reality

What works, what doesn't.

Works well

Reliable

  • USCDI data via Patient Access API
  • Apple Health / Google Health Connect
  • Care Everywhere Epic-to-Epic
  • TEFCA growth accelerating
  • OCR Right of Access Initiative

Still broken

Incomplete

  • DICOM imaging — no certified API
  • Patient portal messages — not in API
  • Billing/financial — uneven
  • Scanned documents — only via EHI Export
  • Cross-provider history depth
  • Genomic data — uneven
  • Mental health — restricted
  • Audit logs — no patient access
  • AI-generated note provenance
Certification provides a floor, not a quality guarantee.

Some vendor FHIR endpoints are fast and complete; others are slow or partial. USCDI elements may be exposed but inconsistently populated. Production access often requires per-hospital onboarding even after vendor approval. Sandbox behavior often diverges from production. Health tech companies should expect to write defensive code.
